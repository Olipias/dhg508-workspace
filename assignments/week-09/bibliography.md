# Bibliography

## Three core scholarly works

### 1. 周敏華. 2009.

**《封診式‧經死》爰書試探** [A Preliminary Examination of Investigation Records in “Jing Si”]. 《東吳中文學報》 17: 1–18. DOI: [10.29460/SJCS.200905.0001](https://doi.org/10.29460/SJCS.200905.0001).

- **Access**: abstract and bibliographic record via Airiti: <https://www.airitilibrary.com/Article/Detail/10271163-200905-x-17-1-18-a>. The journal is open access via TOAJ; the DOI resolves to the article record.
- **Argument**: 《經死》 records a detailed forensic examination procedure, explains the purpose of each examination item, and connects the examination to judicial procedure. The abstract states: “秦代的法醫勘驗工作已相當成熟，且勘驗的過程，也已經與司法程序相結合” (p.1).
- **Use in the project**: the game turns the *Jing si* checklist into a sequence the player must actually execute. The player cannot skip the body examination and submit a verdict; a missing item produces a defective case file and the deputy magistrate rejects it.
- **Precise locators**: p.1 (abstract; the sentence quoted above); pp.1–18 (full article).

### 2. Clyde, Jerremie, Howard Hopkins, and Glenn Wilkinson. 2012.

**“Beyond the ‘Historical’ Simulation: Using Theories of History to Inform Scholarly Game Design.”** *Loading... The Journal of the Canadian Game Studies Association* 6(9): 3–16.

- **Access**: OpenAlex: <https://openalex.org/W131687738>; journal record: <https://loading.journals.publicknowledgeproject.org/index.php/loading/article/view/105>.
- **Argument**: the authors argue for a **gamic mode of history** that constructs historical narrative through **procedural rhetoric**, not merely through accurate reconstruction of the past. The abstract describes “a gamic mode of history that focuses on the construction of the historical narrative via procedural rhetoric,” in which “the reader explores the historical argument through meaningful decision making and play.” The historical argument is carried by the rules and procedures, not by content decoration.
- **Use in the project**: the case-file format, evidence thresholds, and rejection rules are the argument. The player does not read a conclusion; they configure means, rules, and operations to produce a verdict. This is the difference between a “history-themed” game and a game that makes a historical argument through its procedures.
- **Precise locators**: Abstract (quoted phrases); pp.3–16 (full article). The article’s author order is Clyde, Hopkins, Wilkinson; “Clyde & Wilkinson” is a shorthand for the same research group, but the full citation has three authors.

### 3. Gluzman, Renard. 2025.

**“Those Who Do Not Learn History Are Doomed to Play It.”** In *Proceedings of the 3rd International Conference of the ACM Greek SIGCHI Chapter*. ACM. DOI: [10.1145/3749012.3749043](https://doi.org/10.1145/3749012.3749043).

- **Access**: ACM Digital Library: <https://dl.acm.org/doi/full/10.1145/3749012.3749043>.
- **Argument**: historical games rarely engage with history as a discipline; the challenge is to model historical inquiry—framing questions, interpreting sources, and constructing contested narratives—not merely to present historical content. Section 7, “Contestability,” argues that **contestability** should be a structural condition embedded in the game’s epistemological framework, not merely thematic ambiguity. It cites *Return of the Obra Dinn* and *Immortality* as models that simulate reconstruction from fragmentary and often misleading evidence.
- **Use in the project**: the four required evidence items and four optional items can contradict one another; “死难审” is a legitimate but provisional verdict; endings show rejection, reclassification, transfer to the commandery, or a later appeal. The player must act under incomplete evidence and live with a conclusion that may be overturned.
- **Precise locators**: Abstract; §7 “Contestability” (quoted phrase about contestability as a structural condition); §8 “Concluding Remarks” (games should model historical inquiry rather than present content).

## Theoretical background and related work (not one of the three core works)

- Espen J. Aarseth, *Cybertext: Perspectives on Ergodic Literature* (Baltimore: Johns Hopkins University Press, 1997), pp.64–65. Aarseth distinguishes user functions: **interpretative**, **explorative**, **configurative**, and **textonic**. The deck uses “interpretative / configurative” in this precise sense. This is a theoretical footnote, not one of the three core works.
- Jerremie Clyde and Glenn R. Wilkinson, “More Than a Game... Teaching in the Gamic Mode: Disciplinary Knowledge, Digital Literacy, and Collaboration,” *The History Teacher* 46(1) (2012): 45–66. This is the two-author piece on teaching in the gamic mode; the procedural-rhetoric argument cited in the deck is the three-author *Loading...* article above.
